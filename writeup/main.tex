\section{Introduction}

By measuring the positions, brightnesses and motions of stars, we can map out the Milky Way, study its stellar dynamics, and trace its history. Learning the trajectories of stars can also reveal their gravitational interactions with massive systems that may or may not be visible, like black holes and dark matter. We can find patterns in the stars' motions that reveal systems such as globular clusters' tidal tails, which can constrain properties of the Milky Way and surrounding dwarf galaxies.   High-velocity stars, such as hyper-velocity stars, runaway stars, hyper-runaway stars, and fast halo stars, can be used to probe the physical processes and systems responsible for propelling them. In short, proper motions and parallaxes contribute to everything from constructing accurate maps of our Galaxy to uncovering hidden structures.

\section{Existing Data}
Astronomical surveys have historically provided essential data by compiling precise measurements of stellar positions and motions. Each survey has its strengths and limitations, depending on its target, observing techniques, and instrumental sensitivity. Here, we review several foundational surveys, highlighting their contributions and limitations to contextualize our new dataset.

The Hipparcos satellite, launched by ESA, was groundbreaking as the first dedicated astrometry mission, offering proper motions and parallaxes for over 100,000 stars. This dataset, however, was limited to relatively bright stars, with uncertainties rising significantly for fainter objects near its detection limit of \( V \approx 12 \) mag \citep{HIPPARCOS_1997}. %Following this, the Gaia mission has transformed astrometry with precision down to 0.02 mas for over a billion stars up to \( G \approx 21 \) mag in its third data release (DR3), providing an extensive view of stellar motion in the Milky Way \citep{GaiaDR2, GaiaDR3}.

Two all-sky catalogues extended proper motions to many more stars by combining positions from photographic plates spanning many decades. Tycho-2 \citep{Hog2000} combined the Tycho star mapper observations of the Hipparcos satellite with 144 ground-based catalogues, giving proper motions with precisions of $\sim\!2.5$\,mas\,yr$^{-1}$ for the 2.5 million brightest stars ($V \la 12$). USNO-B1.0 \citep{Monet2003} measured positions and proper motions for about a billion objects to $V \approx 21$, from digitised photographic sky-survey plates taken over 50 years.

Ground-based CCD surveys reach several magnitudes fainter than the photographic catalogues, with better photometry, but cover less of the sky. The Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) imaged about a third of the sky in five filters ($ugriz$) to $r \approx 22.2$ \todo{verify: SDSS 95\,per\,cent completeness for point sources} \citep{SDSS_2000}. It observed most of its footprint only once, so on its own it measured proper motions only in the repeatedly observed Stripe~82 region \citep{Bramich_2008}. Elsewhere, SDSS positions have been combined with older catalogues to measure proper motions, for example with USNO-B \citep{Munn2004}.

The Pan-STARRS1 $3\pi$ survey \citep{PanSTARRS_2016} covers the three quarters of the sky north of declination $-30\degr$ in five filters ($grizy$), with about a dozen epochs per filter over four years, reaching $r \approx 23.2$ in the stacked images. Pan-STARRS1 measures proper motions and parallaxes from its own epochs, and its positions have been combined with SDSS and Gaia to extend the time baseline \citep[e.g.][]{Tian_2017}. Like the Legacy Surveys, these are ground-based surveys, and their astrometry is limited by the atmosphere and the detectors rather than by photon noise for bright stars.

Infrared surveys, such as the VISTA Hemisphere Survey (VHS), UKIRT Infrared Deep Sky Survey (UKIDSS), and Two Micron All-Sky Survey (2MASS), provide important data for studying stars obscured by dust. VHS reaches $J \approx 20.2$ \citep{VHS}, and the UKIDSS Large Area Survey $J \approx 19.6$ \citep{UKIDSS_2007}. In contrast, 2MASS, which prioritized all-sky coverage over depth, reaches $J \approx 15.8$ \citep{2MASS_2003} \todo{verify these $J$ depths (Vega) against the survey papers}. However, the lower resolution and sensitivity of these infrared surveys, compared to optical surveys, restrict their precision, particularly for faint objects.

Finally, the Vera Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) will image the southern sky repeatedly for ten years to $r \approx 27$ in its coadded images \citep{Rubin2019}. Measuring the changing sky, including the motions of faint stars, is one of its primary purposes. LSST will use ground-based CCD imaging, like the Legacy Surveys, and will very likely face the same kinds of systematic errors that this paper deals with: differential chromatic refraction, sensor effects such as tree rings, and colour-dependent optical distortions. The methods and lessons of this paper are therefore relevant to LSST as well.

\subsection{DESI Legacy Imaging Surveys} \label{Legacy data}

These surveys avoid regions of high dust extinction, such as the Galactic plane, and thus do not significantly benefit from reduced dust obscuration. The primary advantage of the Legacy Surveys lies in their broad sky coverage and depth, providing a great foundation for proper motion measurements in the optical bands.

The DESI Legacy Imaging Surveys is a project that provides targets for the DESI spectroscopic survey, primarily distant galaxies. It is comprised of three collaborative initiatives: the Dark Energy Camera Legacy Survey, the Beijing-Arizona Sky Survey, and the Mayall z-band Legacy Survey \citep{legacy2019}. For this study, we used the tenth data release (DR10) of the Legacy Surveys, which significantly expands the total footprint to over 20,000 square degrees. This expansion is primarily due to the integration of publicly available DECam imaging from external surveys, including the Dark Energy Survey (DES) \citep{DES}, DELVE \citep{DELVE}, and DECam observations supporting the DeROSITA optical follow-up program \citep{DeROSITA}. These additions complement the original DECaLS data and extend coverage well into the southern sky. DECam data comprise approximately 9,000 square degrees of the DR10 footprint, allowing more detailed observations across both hemispheres. The surveys strategically avoid high dust extinction regions, such as the Galactic plane, so the benefit from reduced dust obscuration is limited. Instead, the advantage lies in their combination of wide area and imaging depth---detecting point sources several magnitudes beyond Gaia's $G \sim 21$ limit \todo{quote DR10 point-source depth from \citet{legacy2019} / DR10 paper}---making them valuable for astrometric applications, particularly proper motion measurements in the optical.

\section{Data Catalogs} 
\subsection{Tractor Catalogs}

The tractor catalogs are derived from the fitting process for source parameters across a set of overlapping images. In cases where a star exhibits variability in brightness, the catalog reflects an average brightness. Gaia stars, however, are treated differently. The catalog assumes the accuracy of Gaia astrometric measurements (RA, Dec, proper motions, parallax) and utilizes these values to predict the star's position (in RA, Dec) for a specific image, considering its timestamp. Notably, we refrain from re-fitting Gaia astrometric measurements; our focus is solely on flux determination during fitting.

For non-Gaia stars (those too faint for Gaia inclusion) and Gaia stars lacking proper motion and parallax data, the catalog does not fit for these attributes and assumes the star remains stationary. The reported RA, Dec for moving stars represents an average position, potentially leading to flux mis-measurements due to the star model being centered inaccurately.

Each entry in the tractor catalogs corresponds to a single source, irrespective of the number of overlapping images in that sky region. Gaia sources are distinguishable by having REF\_CAT = `GE' (indicating ``Gaia EDR3") and possess a REF\_ID value corresponding to the Gaia ``sourceid." For Gaia sources, all astrometric measurements and uncertainties are directly extracted from the Gaia catalog.

\subsection{Forced Photometry Catalogs} \label{sec:forced}

In conventional forced photometry, tractor catalogs are utilised, assuming the correctness of all measured RA and Dec positions. For each image overlapping a specific sky region, the source positions and galaxy shapes are maintained at fixed values, conducting individual fits to determine the optimal flux that best matches each single image. Consequently, if a source exhibits brightness variations, the brightness can be reported in each image rather than as an average.

In this traditional forced photometry approach, all positions remain fixed, preventing the measurement of star motions. This fixed-position strategy streamlines the fitting process, transforming it into a linear least-squares problem, thereby ensuring rapid computations.

To address the challenge of measuring star motions, the forced catalog initially employed a Taylor-expansion concept. In addition to the linear fit for source flux (with fixed positions), it incorporated two linear components representing d(star appearance)/dra and d(star appearance)/ddec. This simultaneous fitting allows the extraction of motion-related information, such as a change in the source's position in the RA direction. The forced photometry processing runs on each CCD image independently, and then the results are compiled into the `forced' data products. 

Although the Taylor expansion measurements may prove acceptable, the catalogue introduced ``full fit" measurements to offer a comprehensive alternative. For each source selected for fitting (specifically those labeled as TYPE=``PSF"), a localized image cutout centered on the source is examined. By subtracting the best model for all other sources, the flux for the target source is isolated. Subsequently, the tractor fitting is performed, enabling the determination of both the source position and the flux. Although more computationally intensive due to nonlinear fitting, this method provides full-fit central RA, Dec, flux, and uncertainties. Notably, it was opted to report only the delta-RA and delta-Dec values compared to the original RA, Dec values. For Gaia stars, the reported RA and Dec values correspond to Gaia-predicted positions at the time the image was captured.

\section{Data Corrections} \label{sec:corrections}

The per-exposure positions from the forced photometry contain several systematic errors that are much larger than their statistical uncertainties for bright stars. We correct four of them, in order: differential chromatic refraction (DCR; Section~\ref{sec:dcr}), CCD-specific distortions (Section~\ref{sec:ccd}), lateral colour (Section~\ref{sec:lateral}), and the zero point of each CCD in each exposure (Section~\ref{sec:zeropoints}). The corrections are applied in sequence: each correction is measured from Gaia stars that have already had the previous corrections applied, so each one only models the residual left by the steps before it. Throughout, Gaia stars serve as the reference. Their positions at the epoch of each exposure, predicted from Gaia astrometry, tell us where each star truly was.

\subsection{Differential Chromatic Refraction} \label{sec:dcr}


Differential Chromatic Refraction (DCR) refers to the phenomenon where the apparent position of an astronomical object varies with its color or wavelength. This effect is caused by the Earth's atmosphere acting as a prism, dispersing light from celestial objects into different colors or wavelengths as it passes through the atmosphere. DCR becomes particularly significant when observing near the horizon, where the light from celestial objects passes through a thicker layer of the Earth's atmosphere, and for observations in bluer wavelengths.

To first order, the DCR shift of a star is proportional to $\tan z$ and to the star's colour \citep{Bernstein_2017}:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \mathbf{x} = K_b \, c \, \tan z \; \hat{\mathbf{p}},
    \label{eq:dcr_linear}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta \mathbf{x}$ is the apparent shift of a star, $c$ is its colour, $z$ is the zenith angle, $\hat{\mathbf{p}}$ is the unit vector on the sky pointing towards the zenith (i.e.\ along the parallactic angle), and $K_b$ is a constant for each filter band $b$ that depends on the instrumental bandpass and the refractive index of the atmosphere. We find that the data require a more flexible dependence on colour (below).

For every measurement of every star, we compute the hour angle at the middle of the exposure from the exposure time stamp, the star's position, and the longitude of Cerro Tololo, and from it the airmass $X$ and parallactic angle. We use $\tan z = \sqrt{X^2 - 1}$, which holds for a plane-parallel atmosphere. We then rotate the measured offset of the star (the difference between its fitted position in that exposure and its catalogue position; Section~\ref{sec:forced}) into the frame aligned with the parallactic angle, so that the component along $\hat{\mathbf{p}}$ contains the DCR signal. The perpendicular component should contain no DCR signal, and we use it as a check.

An important subtlety is that the Community Pipeline astrometric solution for each CCD is fit to Gaia reference stars. It therefore already absorbs the DCR shift experienced by a star with the \emph{mean colour of those reference stars}. Only a star's colour relative to that reference colour produces a residual shift. We therefore use a relative colour
\begin{equation}
    c = (g - i) - (g - i)_{\rm ref},
\end{equation}
where $g-i$ is the star's colour from the Tractor catalogue fluxes and $(g-i)_{\rm ref}$ is the mean colour of the astrometric reference stars on that CCD in that exposure. We estimate the latter from the mean Gaia $G_{\rm BP} - G_{\rm RP}$ colour of the reference stars, converted with the linear relation $(g-i) = -0.56 + 1.34\,(G_{\rm BP}-G_{\rm RP})$ \todo{source of this colour transformation}. We only correct stars with $-2 < c < 3$\,mag.

About 14\,per\,cent of point sources have no measured $g-i$, mostly because their brick has no $i$-band imaging. For these we predict $g-i$ from $g-z$, which tracks it closely along the stellar locus: a cubic fitted to stars with signal-to-noise ratio above 50 in all four bands predicts $g-i$ with a robust scatter of 0.025\,mag and a bias below 0.025\,mag at all colours, for $0 < g-i < 3.2$. Other colours are much poorer predictors ($g-r$, $r-z$ and Gaia $G_{\rm BP} - G_{\rm RP}$ give 0.12--0.17\,mag, with colour-dependent biases of up to 0.27\,mag), so we use no other fallback. Stars with neither colour are not corrected and are flagged (see Section~\ref{sec:flags}).

To measure the colour dependence, we take Gaia stars with positional uncertainties below 10\,mas, before any correction, and compute the median offset along $\hat{\mathbf{p}}$ in cells of relative colour and $\tan z$ ($\sim\!80$ million measurements in RA 0--63\degr). The offsets scale linearly with $\tan z$ in every band, but not linearly with colour: the slope of offset against colour is steeper for blue stars than for red ones in $g$, and the reverse in $r$ and $i$. The relative colour distribution of the calibration stars is bimodal, with blue main-sequence turn-off stars and red K and M dwarfs, and the change of slope falls between the two populations. At fixed colour, however, the DCR shift does not depend on population \todo{one sentence on the H6 test}, so we model it as a function of colour alone, with two straight lines that meet at a break colour $c_b$:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \mathbf{x} = \tan z \,\left[\, a_b + \beta_b^{\rm blue} \min(c - c_b, 0) + \beta_b^{\rm red} \max(c - c_b, 0) \,\right] \hat{\mathbf{p}},
    \label{eq:dcr}
\end{equation}
with $c$ clipped to $[-1.5, 2.5]$\,mag. We fit the four parameters of each band to the cell medians, weighted by their uncertainties, choosing $c_b$ on a grid (Table~\ref{tab:dcr}). On cells from bricks held out of the fit, this model describes the medians with a weighted rms of 1.25, 0.55, 0.42 and 0.43\,mas in $g$, $r$, $i$ and $z$, as well as or better than polynomials in colour up to fifth order and better than a single linear slope. The correction is subtracted along $\hat{\mathbf{p}}$ and converted to pixel units using the DECam pixel scale of $0.262$\,arcsec\,pixel$^{-1}$. The $i$ and $z$ corrections are small but not negligible ($\sim\!3$ and $\sim\!1$\,mas at $\tan z = 1$ for a typical star).

\subsection{CCD-Specific Distortions} \label{sec:ccd}
In DECam CCDs, photons in the g, r, and i bands generate electron-hole pairs near the CCD surface, and the holes (charge carriers) must drift through the $250 \mu m$-thick silicon substrate to reach the pixel electrodes \citep{Bernstein_2017}. Ideally, this movement is guided by a vertical electric field; however, imperfections in the silicon or manufacturing process can create transverse electric fields that deflect the holes sideways. This lateral drift causes an astrometric shift, where the detected position of a photon appears offset, introducing systematic positional errors in astronomical observations. 

When comparing expected positions from Gaia and the observed positions, two main sources of distortions can be seen. The first is tree rings, which are caused by fluctuations in the impurity density of silicon boules \citep{Bernstein_2017}. As the name suggests, this effect looks like rings of up-and-down (or left- and right-) shifts, with the centers of the rings vary per CCD. The second source of distortions is a combination of the edges of CCDs, and the tape and screws holding the CCDs in place. 

% In the $g$, $r$, and $i$ bands, photons create voids near the surface of the DECam CCD, necessitating a traversal of the 250 µm thickness before being captured by the pixels \citep{Bernstein_2017}. Any electric field components perpendicular to the surface cause charge carriers to drift laterally, inducing an apparent shift in astrometry. Two notable sources of these stray fields in DECam CCDs are identified. ``Tree rings" result from impurity density fluctuations in the silicon boules from which the CCD wafers are cut. The zone refining process creates circular symmetry about the boule axis, and the wafers are cut perpendicular to this axis, leading to irregular oscillating ring patterns as astrometric distortions. The ring centers may be on or off the device edges, and the distortions are visible in flat-field images due to oscillations in the solid angle of sky received by each pixel \citep{Bernstein_2017}. While these distortions are specific to the characteristics of the CCD, their magnitude is contingent on the filter used during the exposure since the tree-ring effects depend on how deep the photons go into the silicon before being converted to photo-electrons.

% TODO need this comparison with Berstein?
To correct for these distortions, \cite{Bernstein_2017} modeled the tree rings by identifying the ring center for each CCD through visual examination of the flat-field images. Subsequently, they generated templates of the expected astrometric distortion around this center from a high-pass-filtered, azimuthally averaged profile of the flat-field signal \citep{Bernstein_2017}.

In the case of this paper, we used Gaia data to empirically create distortion maps, which we then used to correct the positions of objects in the CCDs. Starting from the DCR-corrected positions, we binned the CCD pixels into $16 \times 16$\,pixel bins ($128 \times 256$ bins per CCD) and stored the median difference between the observed position and the position expected from Gaia in each of the $x$ and $y$ directions. Because the magnitude of the effect depends on how deep photons penetrate the silicon, and therefore on the filter, we created a separate distortion map for each of the 61 CCDs and each filter. We used Gaia stars with positional uncertainties below 10\,mas, no data-quality flags, a colour (so that their DCR correction is reliable), and not affected by bright stars or saturation: 294 million measurements in RA 0--63\degr, a median of 28--45 per bin depending on the band.

The bin size trades resolution against noise. We built maps from 80\,per\,cent of the bricks and measured, on the remaining 20\,per\,cent, the robust per-axis scatter of the corrected positions. With $16$-pixel bins the scatter drops from 14.6, 13.1, 12.4 and 11.3\,mas to 12.4, 10.8, 9.9 and 9.6\,mas in $g$, $r$, $i$ and $z$, removing 6--8\,mas in quadrature in every band. Coarser bins remove less ($32$ pixels: 5.8--7.3\,mas; $64$ pixels: 5.2--6.2\,mas), so the tree rings have structure on scales below 32 pixels that the finer bins capture despite their noise. The maps used for the catalogue are built from all bricks.

\subsection{Lateral Colour} \label{sec:lateral}
Lateral colour in DECam is caused by chromatic aberration in its wide-field corrector optics: light of different wavelengths is refracted by slightly different amounts, which produces wavelength-dependent positional offsets on the focal plane. The offset is mostly radial and grows towards the edge of the field of view (Figure~\ref{fig:lateral}). Within a single band, stars of different colours have different effective wavelengths, so the shift depends on a star's colour.

The CCD distortion maps of Section~\ref{sec:ccd} are built from all Gaia stars regardless of colour, so they already remove the lateral-colour shift of a star of typical colour. What remains is the colour-dependent part. Chromatic aberration is a smooth optical effect across the focal plane, so we model it with a few parameters per band rather than with binned maps:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \mathbf{x}_{\rm lat} = (c - c_0)\,\left[ P^x_b(u, v),\, P^y_b(u, v) \right],
    \label{eq:lateral}
\end{equation}
where $c = g - i$ (measured, or predicted from $g-z$; Section~\ref{sec:dcr}), $(u, v)$ is the position in the focal plane, and $P^x_b$ and $P^y_b$ are independent two-dimensional polynomials of total order 3 (10 coefficients each) for band $b$. The pivot colour $c_0$ is the colour at which the correction vanishes. Since the CCD maps remove the shift of the median star in each bin, we set $c_0 = 1.469$\,mag, the median $g-i$ of the stars the maps were built from. We fit the coefficients to the residuals of the same Gaia stars after the DCR and CCD corrections, with the median residual of each CCD and band subtracted, by iteratively $3\sigma$-clipped least squares. An earlier version with binned maps per unit colour was dominated by noise and increased the scatter.

On bricks held out of the fit, the correction reduces the robust per-axis scatter of Gaia-star positions from 12.47 to 11.84\,mas in $g$, and by less than 0.1\,mas in $r$, $i$ and $z$: lateral colour matters mainly in $g$, where the applied shift has an rms of 6.3\,mas (1.6, 0.9 and 0.4\,mas in $r$, $i$ and $z$). A separate pivot colour per band gives no further improvement. The correction is subtracted from every measurement.

\subsection{Exposure Zero Points} \label{sec:zeropoints}

The three corrections above are static models: each depends on a star's colour, airmass, or position on the detector or in the focal plane, and is the same for every exposure. After applying them, the stars on one CCD in one exposure still share a common offset from their Gaia-predicted positions. We call this the zero point of that CCD in that exposure. We found it through its effect on proper motions: the proper motions of Gaia stars in the same brick differ from Gaia's in the same direction, by $\sim\!0.5$\,mas\,yr$^{-1}$ beyond what their individual errors allow, and on average our proper motions were offset from Gaia's by $-0.2$ and $+0.3$\,mas\,yr$^{-1}$ in right ascension and declination. A shared positional offset of a few mas per exposure produces exactly this: stars observed together inherit the same errors, so their proper motions err together, and a slow drift of the offsets with time adds a spurious proper motion common to every star.

We measure the zero point of every CCD in every exposure as the median offset, after the previous corrections, of the bright Gaia stars on it: stars with positional uncertainties below 10\,mas, a full five-parameter Gaia astrometric solution, and no bright-star or saturation flags. In RA 0--63\degr, this gives zero points for 3.15 million CCD-exposures (58\,445 exposures), from 290 million measurements, with a median of 74 stars each. The zero points scatter by 2.2\,mas in right ascension and 4.2\,mas in declination, beyond the 1.2\,mas expected from measurement noise alone. They are larger in $g$ (3.7 and 5.2\,mas) than in $z$ (1.5 and 3.5\,mas), and vary mostly from CCD to CCD within an exposure (2.2 and 3.6\,mas) rather than as a whole exposure (1.1 and 2.4\,mas). This is consistent with atmospheric turbulence, which displaces stars coherently over arcminute scales within an exposure \citep{Bernstein_2017}, together with residual errors in the astrometric solution of each CCD. Averaged over all exposures, the zero points also drift with time, by $-0.20$\,mas\,yr$^{-1}$ in right ascension and $+0.12$\,mas\,yr$^{-1}$ in declination between 2013 and 2021: a spurious proper motion of the whole reference frame, matching the mean offset of our proper motions from Gaia's. \todo{Ask Dustin which Gaia release and reference epoch the CP astrometry used for each survey; a change over the years could explain the drift.}

We subtract each CCD's zero point from the positions of every object measured on that CCD in that exposure. For the 1\,per\,cent of CCD-exposures with fewer than 10 such Gaia stars, we use the median zero point of the other CCDs in the exposure (0.9\,per\,cent), or apply no correction if fewer than three CCDs have one (0.2\,per\,cent). Because the correction is measured on Gaia stars, comparing the corrected proper motions of the same stars with Gaia would be partly circular. We therefore validated it by splitting the Gaia stars into two random halves, measuring zero points from each half, and correcting each star with the zero points of the other half only. For 177\,000 clean Gaia stars with $16 < G < 19.5$ in two RA slices, the cross-validated correction removes the mean offset from Gaia (from $-0.23$ and $+0.26$ to $+0.03$ and $+0.01$\,mas\,yr$^{-1}$), reduces the scatter of our proper motions about Gaia's from 1.56 to 1.52\,mas\,yr$^{-1}$ in right ascension and from 1.81 to 1.64\,mas\,yr$^{-1}$ in declination, and reduces the part of that scatter shared by stars in the same brick from 0.47 to 0.36 and from 0.55 to 0.32\,mas\,yr$^{-1}$. Fainter Gaia stars ($19.5 < G < 21$), which contribute little to the zero points, improve similarly (declination scatter 2.28 to 2.13\,mas\,yr$^{-1}$). The cross-validated and full corrections give the same results to within 0.04\,mas\,yr$^{-1}$, so the circularity is negligible in practice. \todo{Re-measure on the final catalogue; consider a figure of zero points vs.\ time and across the focal plane.}

Tying each exposure's astrometry to Gaia in this way places all our proper motions, including those of stars far fainter than Gaia's limit, in the Gaia reference frame. The remaining field-coherent error ($\sim\!0.3$\,mas\,yr$^{-1}$) may come from structure on scales smaller than a CCD, which this correction cannot remove.

\section{Calculating Proper Motions and Parallaxes} \label{sec:pm_method}

\subsection{Astrometric model}

For each star we have a set of $N$ measurements, one per exposure, across all filters. Measurement $i$ consists of the corrected offsets $(\Delta\alpha_{*,i}, \Delta\delta_i)$ of the star's position from its Tractor catalogue position, after the corrections of Section~\ref{sec:corrections}, where $\Delta\alpha_* = \Delta\alpha\cos\delta$, and the exposure epoch $t_i$. Because the Tractor position is a time-averaged position, the offsets are small, and the astrometric model is linear in its parameters. We fit two models. The first contains only a positional offset and a proper motion:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \Delta\alpha_{*,i} &= \Delta\alpha_{*,0} + \mu_{\alpha*}\,(t_i - t_{\rm ref}), \\
    \Delta\delta_i &= \Delta\delta_0 + \mu_{\delta}\,(t_i - t_{\rm ref}).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:pm_model}
\end{equation}
The second adds a parallax $\varpi$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \Delta\alpha_{*,i} &= \Delta\alpha_{*,0} + \mu_{\alpha*}\,(t_i - t_{\rm ref}) + \varpi\,P_{\alpha,i}, \\
    \Delta\delta_i &= \Delta\delta_0 + \mu_{\delta}\,(t_i - t_{\rm ref}) + \varpi\,P_{\delta,i},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:plx_model}
\end{equation}
where $(P_{\alpha,i}, P_{\delta,i})$ are the parallax factors: the displacement, at the star's position and epoch $t_i$, of a star with unit parallax. We compute these with the \emph{legacypipe} routine \texttt{radec\_at\_mjd}.

We solve both models by weighted linear least squares. The design matrix is weighted by the per-measurement uncertainties (below), and the parameter covariance is $(\mathbf{A}^{\rm T}\mathbf{A})^{-1}$ for the weighted design matrix $\mathbf{A}$. We report its diagonal as inverse variances. The fitted offsets $(\Delta\alpha_{*,0}, \Delta\delta_0)$ are added to the Tractor position to give an updated position at the reference epoch.

Sources with a reference-catalogue astrometric solution (in practice Gaia stars, with Gaia DR3 proper motion, parallax and reference epoch J2016.0) are treated differently by the forced photometry: in each image, the source is placed at its Gaia-predicted position at that epoch, including proper motion and parallax. The measured offsets of these sources are therefore residuals with respect to the Gaia astrometric solution, and the fit above yields $\Delta\mu$ and $\Delta\varpi$ relative to Gaia. To make the catalogue uniform, we add the Gaia values back, $\mu = \mu_{\rm Gaia} + \Delta\mu$ and $\varpi = \varpi_{\rm Gaia} + \Delta\varpi$, adding the Gaia variances to ours. The updated position is the Gaia position propagated to $t_{\rm ref}$ (proper motion only), plus the fitted offset. All proper motions, parallaxes and positions in our catalogue are thus absolute, while the original Tractor columns (including the Gaia \texttt{pmra}, \texttt{pmdec} and \texttt{parallax}) are kept unchanged. A star's measurement relative to Gaia is simply the difference of the two.

We choose the reference epoch $t_{\rm ref}$ for each star as the inverse-variance-weighted mean epoch of its measurements. This choice decorrelates the positional offset from the proper motion. Because of it, $t_{\rm ref}$ differs from star to star and from the Gaia DR3 reference epoch (J2016.0), so we store it in the catalogue (\texttt{ref\_mjd}).

\subsection{Measurement uncertainties}

The forced-photometry fit provides a statistical uncertainty for each position. As shown in Section~\ref{sec:corr_analysis}, for bright stars this greatly underestimates the true scatter, which levels off at a systematic floor. We therefore add a systematic term in quadrature to each coordinate:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_i^2 = \sigma_{{\rm stat},i}^2 + \sigma_{{\rm sys},b}^2,
\end{equation}
with $\sigma_{\rm sys}$ = 16, 12, 12 and 10\,mas in $g$, $r$, $i$ and $z$ \todo{update: the error model is being refitted after the exposure zero points (Section~\ref{sec:zeropoints}), with a systematic term that grows with airmass and an extra term along the refraction direction, which correlates the right-ascension and declination errors of each measurement}. Without this term, bright stars would receive unrealistically large weights, and their fitted proper-motion uncertainties would be far too small.

We calibrated this error model with the residuals of the fits themselves. For each clean point source we fit a straight line (equation~\ref{eq:pm_model}) and divide each measurement's residual by its uncertainty, giving a pull; if the uncertainties are right, the pulls have unit width at every magnitude. Writing $\sigma_i^2 = (k\,\sigma_{{\rm stat},i})^2 + \sigma_{{\rm sys},b}^2$, we find $k$ and $\sigma_{\rm sys}$ per band that give a robust pull width of one in every magnitude bin, using 23 million measurements of 0.7 million stars. The best fit has $k = 1$ in every band (0.9 in $i$): faint stars, whose errors are dominated by photon noise, have pulls of width 0.90--1.02 in both coordinates, so the statistical uncertainties of the forced photometry are correct. The systematic term sets the bright end. The robust width describes the core of the distribution; the tails are heavier than Gaussian, with 1--7\,per\,cent of measurements more than $5\sigma$ from their star's fit (more for faint stars), spread evenly over exposures. We keep these measurements and flag stars where they are common (Section~\ref{sec:flags}). \todo{PM pull widths of clean Gaia stars in the final catalogue; and whether the excess scatter along the parallactic direction (Dec pulls $\sim$10--20\,per\,cent above RA for bright stars) needs its own term.}

This test also revealed a problem in the input uncertainties: forced photometry produced before September 2026 divided the declination inverse variance by $\cos^2\delta$, as is appropriate only for right ascension, making declination uncertainties too small by a factor $\cos\delta$ (2.4 at $\delta = -65\degr$). It was fixed in \emph{legacypipe} (commit \texttt{7aece15}), with a header keyword marking corrected files. For inputs produced earlier, identified by that keyword or by their creation date, we multiply the declination inverse variance by $\cos^2\delta$. With this correction the faint-star pulls are independent of declination, and equal in both coordinates.

\subsection{Measurements used, and quality flags} \label{sec:flags}

We fit every source classified as a point source (Tractor \texttt{TYPE = PSF}). This includes Gaia stars, which we use for validation, as well as fainter point sources not detected by Gaia. Our approach is to use only what the inputs trust, and to flag rather than remove what may be unreliable.

\paragraph*{Measurements.} A measurement enters a star's fit only if the forced photometry measured it: it must have a positive inverse variance in both coordinates and a non-zero fitted offset. In a few per cent of epochs the forced photometry reports an uncertainty but no measured offset \todo{fraction in the final sample}. These epochs carry no positional information, and including them would pull the fit towards zero motion, so we exclude them. No measurement is rejected for its residual: measurements far from the fit are kept, and counted (see \texttt{BAD\_EPOCHS} below).

\paragraph*{When no fit is made.} The five-parameter model needs at least three measurements ($2N \geq 5$ equations), with distinct epochs. We therefore attempt a fit only for stars with $N \geq 3$ measured epochs and a non-zero time baseline, and we treat a fit as failed if its design matrix has a condition number above $10^8$ (e.g.\ when all epochs fall on very few nights). These are the only cases in which a point source has no proper motion in the catalogue.

\paragraph*{Quality flags.} Every other condition that can make a proper motion unreliable is recorded in a bitmask, \texttt{pm\_quality} (Table~\ref{tab:pm_quality}), and the star keeps its fit. Throughout this paper we use only \emph{clean} stars, those with $\texttt{pm\_quality} = 0$; users of the catalogue can choose their own selection. The thresholds were set by comparing our proper motions of Gaia stars with Gaia's. Among stars with fewer than 10 measured epochs, or a baseline shorter than two years, 10 and 26\,per\,cent respectively differ from Gaia by more than 30\,mas\,yr$^{-1}$, compared with 0.13\,per\,cent of clean stars \todo{re-measure on the final catalogue}. For bad epochs, about 1--7\,per\,cent of all measurements lie more than $5\sigma$ from their star's fit, spread evenly over exposures, so flagging any star with a single such epoch would mostly flag stars with many epochs. Flagging stars for which at least 10\,per\,cent of epochs are bad flags 4.7\,per\,cent of clean Gaia stars, yet catches 89\,per\,cent of those differing from Gaia by more than 30\,mas\,yr$^{-1}$.

\subsection{Catalogue contents} \label{sec:columns}
